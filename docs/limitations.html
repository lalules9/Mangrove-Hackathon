QAIRI, Queensland Artificial Intelligence Risk Index

Limitations

This document outlines any limitations in the design and development of the QAIRI.

What the index does not claim to be

The main limitation

Most of the index is not AI-specific in its mechanism

Essential services, population vulnerability and institutional capacity would score a council the same way for a natural disaster as for an AI-caused one. They measure who absorbs any shock badly, an approach inspired by the ADRI. We build on the legitimacy of the ADRI with an AI framing on top.

Of the six scored components, synthetic warning is the only one whose scored factor genuinely depends on AI as the cause, a fabricated AI-generated warning message during a live emergency. Deployment evidence and systemic labour are about AI specifically, but they measure adoption and exposure, not a mechanism by which AI itself causes harm.

Counter-argument, addressed directly

Pabai v Commonwealth (2025)

In 2025 the Federal Court declined to find that the Commonwealth owed Torres Strait Islanders a duty of care over climate change harm. This project's legal grounding (see Legal background) rests on a narrower, different claim: that an AI-caused failure of a specific essential service is a concrete, attributable "event" under s16(1)(d) of the Disaster Management Act, not that government owes a general duty to prevent diffuse, hard-to-attribute harm. Climate-change causation is diffuse across decades and emitters; an AI-caused water or power outage in a named council area is not. Pabai is still worth naming, since Aurukun and the Torres Strait councils sit at the highest-risk end of this index: a court has already declined, in a case involving these same communities, to extend an affirmative duty over harm resembling this project's broader premise, even though the narrower statutory hook this project actually relies on is not the claim that case rejected.

Rogue AI is deliberately excluded

Not every Hendrycks category is represented, and one is missing on purpose

Rogue AI, an AI system developing its own goal and resisting correction, requires a level of autonomous capability nothing in this dataset has. A pothole detector or a warning system that fails or is hacked is a malfunction or a case of malicious use, not rogue AI. Including it as a scored factor would mean inventing a proxy for something that is not actually present in any confirmed deployment in this dataset, so it stays named and excluded rather than forced in.

Data quality

Confirmed AI deployment is press-derived and sparse

Only 8 of 78 councils have confirmed AI use, found by searching for news coverage. The other 70 are not confirmed to be free of AI use, they are simply not covered in the press the same way. The index treats "no evidence found" as missing data, not as evidence of absence, and excludes those councils from that component's score rather than scoring them as safe.

Disaster frequency, corrected during this build

The first version of this input matched disaster events to a council by coordinates pulled from a public disaster events catalogue, and that approach was wrong more often than it was right. Many entries shared the exact same generic coordinate near a major regional town rather than the real event location, and it missed real, confirmed events entirely, including Cyclone Jasper at Wujal Wujal and Cyclone Kirrily's actual path.

Replaced with the Queensland Reconstruction Authority's own Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements activation records, filterable by council directly on QRA's site, every activation since 2010 to 2011. This is the authoritative source, not an approximation of one, and every council's count is independently checkable.

Systemic labour is one occupational proxy, not a validated exposure index

Which occupations count as AI-exposed (Professionals, Clerical and Administrative Workers, Sales Workers) is a single judgement call about ANZSCO major groups, not a published, validated AI-exposure index, and it has not been cross-checked against a second published exposure measure.

AI-exposed occupations are concentrated in wealthy, high-capacity metro councils, the same councils that score well on ADRI. This component therefore pulls in the opposite direction from the rest of the index, and is a large part of why the overall correlation against ADRI moved further from zero once it was added. Whether a high AI-exposed-occupation share is a risk or a proxy for capacity is an open question. The index scores it as a risk; it could equally be read as a proxy for capacity.

Queensland council staff and finance figures are 2015 to 2016

Every resource in the state's comparative information open data release stops there, whatever the portal's last updated date says. Current years exist only as documents on the department's site, which blocks automated fetching. Columns carry their own vintage field so the age of the number travels with it.

ADRI's own LGA aggregation is area-weighted, not population-weighted

The source ADRI data is published at SA2 level and aggregated to LGA by area times share, because the population fields in the public API response are null. Area weighting over-weights large, empty SA2s. Joining ABS population estimates by SA2 code and swapping the weight would be more accurate, and was not done in the time available.

Methodological choices worth contesting

Weights are expert judgement, not fitted or independently validated

The 22/18/25/15/10/10 split reflects reasoned judgement about which signals matter most and which rest on the thinnest evidence, not a statistical fit to data or an external panel's ranking. The interactive map's sliders let a reader test whether the ranking survives a different weighting, rather than presenting these numbers as optimised.

Vendor concentration is not measured

A single vendor outage affecting many councils at once has no natural hazard analogue and would be one of the cleanest sources of genuine divergence from ADRI. The one data source tried for it, a specific contract disclosure field, had no variance across all 78 councils and could not be scored. Measuring it properly would mean manually reading each council's published contract register, roughly a day of work, judged not worth it against the time available for this hackathon.

The AI policy scan is not complete

47 of 78 council websites had been checked for a published AI governance policy as of this submission. The remaining 31 are disproportionately small, remote councils, which are also the least likely to have an indexed policy to find. Diminishing returns, not an oversight.

Summary. This index measures general council vulnerability, using an approach inspired by a validated natural-hazard framework, with a small number of genuinely AI-specific signals layered on top. It diverges from Australia's own disaster resilience index by a real, reported margin, not a manufactured one. What it is not is a complete or validated measure of catastrophic AI risk. Nobody has built one of those yet, for any jurisdiction.
