Before the list

Three things that should change what you build

01

The theme is catastrophic risk, not civic tech

Mangrove is an AI safety field-building organisation. The brief is how local governments should respond to AI-driven catastrophic risks. A tool that makes council services friendlier will read as off-theme. The work has to connect to severe, systemic, or irreversible harm — and to say honestly how large that harm plausibly is.

02

The criteria are research criteria

Question and scope. Rigor and honesty. Usefulness to decisionmakers. Communication to intelligent non-specialists. Nothing on that list rewards technical difficulty. Strong technical teams lose these hackathons by shipping an impressive demo that answers no stated question and states no limitations.

03

Your asymmetry is larger than you think

Judges span digital governance, AI research, economics and AI safety. They can all read code. Almost none of them can tell you what happens to a person in a remote community when an automated decision goes against them. Julie can. Build the thing that only your team could have built.

The Australian gift. Australia has the world's best-documented case of an automated government system causing catastrophic harm at population scale: Robodebt, and a Royal Commission report that names exactly how it happened. Every country has an equivalent scandal; Australia is the one with a forensic public record. That record is a free, rigorous, globally legible foundation — and it is sitting in a PDF that nobody at this hackathon except you will think to use.

The three-year ledger

Counted from the Government's own tracking table. Three questions get three different answers, and the gap between them is where the work is.

Accepted56/57Accepted or accepted in principle, Nov 2023. The 57th was rejected.
Reported done5251 “Implemented” plus one “Implemented – ongoing”.
Still open416.2, 17.1, 17.2, 18.2 — after nearly three years.
Needing law, unpassed3Every remaining item the Government itself flags as requiring legislation.

Two of those three unlegislated recommendations are the automated decision-making ones — 17.1, a consistent legal framework for automation in government with a clear review path and published business rules; and 17.2, a body empowered to monitor and audit automated decisions for bias and fairness. Both remain at “the Government will consider”. The third, 18.2, is the six-year limitation on debt recovery, accepted only in principle.

The distinction that carries the argument. Most of the 52 completed items were delivered administratively — training, directions, guidance, Budget Process Operational Rules, recordkeeping standards, legal practice standards. The genuinely legislative wins sit in chapters 20 and 21 and rode on the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024: the ART itself, publication of first-instance decisions, the re-established Administrative Review Council, and the Ombudsman's referral powers. So: the accountability plumbing was rebuilt. The rules governing the machine were not.

And none of it reaches a council

Every one of the 57 recommendations is directed at Commonwealth entities — Services Australia, DSS, the ATO, AGD, the APSC, the Ombudsman, the tribunal. A royal commission into a Commonwealth scheme produced Commonwealth remedies, and local government is a creature of state legislation. The result is a clean, checkable gap:

Federal Privacy Act
New APP 1.7–1.9 require disclosure of automated decisions in privacy policies from 10 December 2026 — the closest thing Australia has to a Robodebt-derived ADM transparency law. Councils are state authorities and sit outside the Act, so it binds no Australian council.
NSW · VIC · QLD · TAS · NT
State privacy Acts do cover local councils — but they are collection, use and disclosure principles written before ADM was a live issue. I found no ADM-specific provision in any of them. Verify this yourself before you put it on a slide; it is the load-bearing claim.
Western Australia
The Privacy and Responsible Information Sharing Act 2024 commences 1 July 2026 and does cover local and regional governments — 11 information privacy principles, breach reporting from 1 January 2027. Again, no ADM provision.
South Australia
No privacy legislation at all. SA councils are covered by no privacy statute, let alone an ADM one.
AI policy instruments
The Commonwealth's responsible-AI policy binds non-corporate Commonwealth entities. The NSW AI Assessment Framework binds NSW government agencies. Councils are separate bodies under state local government Acts and sit outside both perimeters.
The evidence that the gap is live. On 8 March 2024 the NSW Ombudsman tabled a special report mapping 275 automated decision-making systems across NSW state agencies and local councils — the first systematic mapping of its kind in Australia. Roughly a third of the systems reported were in development, being piloted, or planned within three years, mostly by local councils. Neither agencies nor councils have any obligation to report ADM use at all, and the Ombudsman warned that voluntary reporting will not be sufficient. Councils are among the fastest-growing adopters of automated decision-making in the one jurisdiction anybody has counted — and they sit outside every reform Robodebt produced.

This sharpens project 01 considerably. The question stops being would today's frameworks have caught Robodebt? and becomes the harder, better one: for local government there is no framework to catch it, the two recommendations that would have built one are the two still unlegislated three years on, and councils are adopting automation faster than the state agencies that are regulated. That is a finding, not a checklist.

The catalogue

Ten projects

Each one leads with the question it answers, because that is the first thing being judged.

01

The Robodebt Test

Govern Difficulty: Low–Med Recommended

“Robodebt produced 57 recommendations. Three years on, the two that would have regulated automated decision-making are still unlegislated — and none of the reform reaches local government. What would a council-grade test have to look like?”

What you build

  • Read the Robodebt Royal Commission final report with Claude Code and extract a structured list of failure modes — no lawful basis established before scaling; a statistical average substituted for a fact about an individual; the onus of disproof shifted onto the affected person; internal legal doubt suppressed; complaints handled as customer service rather than treated as a harm signal; no pilot before population-scale rollout; no pathway that could actually reverse a decision.
  • Convert each failure mode into a testable screening criterion — a question with a defensible pass/fail, traceable back to a numbered finding.
  • Score real, current governance artifacts against the criteria: the NSW AI Assessment Framework, the Commonwealth DTA policy for responsible AI use in government, Australia's eight AI Ethics Principles, ISO/IEC 42001, plus three to five actual council AI policies you can find online. Publish the scoring matrix and the rubric.
  • Ship the screener itself: a 12-question, 45-minute pre-deployment check a council officer can run without a consultant, where every question cites the finding that produced it.

Data required

All public, all free, no scraping: the Royal Commission final report (PDF), the framework documents, and council AI policies published on council websites. Zero data risk — this is the single biggest reason it fits one day.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Makes a claim, not just a tool. The likely finding — that most frameworks would have passed Robodebt — is a genuine, checkable result.
  • Uniquely Australian, but immediately legible to judges anywhere; every country has its own version.
  • Julie's social work background makes the harm analysis authoritative rather than assumed.
  • Risk: if done lazily it is “a checklist”. Defend against this by scoring real named artifacts, publishing the rubric, and being explicit where a framework partially passes.
02

Shockmap AU

Prepare Measure Difficulty: Medium

“Which Australian local government areas face the largest AI labour shock — and are they the ones least able to absorb it?”

What you build

  • Pull ABS Census occupation counts by LGA, map ANZSCO occupations to a published AI-exposure index, and produce an exposure score for each of Australia's ~537 LGAs.
  • Overlay three absorption variables: SEIFA relative disadvantage, remoteness classification, and council own-source revenue share — a council that funds itself from rates on a single industry town has a very different shock profile from an inner-metro council.
  • Output an interactive choropleth, a ranked table, and a one-page auto-generated brief per LGA that a general manager could take to a council meeting.
  • Run the whole pipeline across three different exposure indices and publish where they disagree. This sensitivity analysis is the rigor score.

Data required

ABS Census DataPacks or the ABS Data Explorer API; SEIFA; ASGS remoteness; published exposure indices; state grants commission or council annual report financials. All free, but the ANZSCO-to-index crosswalk is fiddly and the newest usable Census is 2021 — 2026 Census data is not out yet.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Produces a number that does not currently exist at LGA resolution. Visually the strongest artifact of the ten.
  • Answers Prepare and Measure simultaneously, with a clean quantitative spine.
  • Risk: exposure indices are genuinely contested and the crosswalk is lossy. Treat that as the honest core of the work, not a footnote.
  • Risk: a technical team may attempt something similar, and labour disruption sits at the edge of “catastrophic”. Frame it as a state capacity shock — demand for council services rises exactly as the rates base falls.
03

Last Mile

Measure Difficulty: Medium Recommended

“When a resident asks a general AI assistant about their council, how often is the answer right — and does that depend on which council they live in?”

What you build

  • Don't test council chatbots. Most councils don't have one, and remote Indigenous councils certainly don't. Test what is already happening: people ask ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and AI search about their council today, at scale, with no council involvement and no council control. That is measurable right now, for any council in Australia, without anyone's permission.
  • A fixed question set every council can answer factually — bin collection day, rates due date and hardship process, dog registration fee, how to lodge a development application, tip opening hours, how to contest an infringement, who the mayor is.
  • Ground truth from each council's own website, scraped and human-spot-checked. This is what makes it scoreable rather than impressionistic.
  • A cohort of Queensland's 77 councils, stratified: south-east metro → regional city → rural shire → remote → the 17 Indigenous councils (16 Aboriginal Shire Councils plus Torres Strait Island Regional Council).
  • Score per council and per stratum: correct, wrong, confidently wrong, refused, and “answered about a different council”. Then plot accuracy against remoteness and council size.

Data required

A list of QLD councils and their websites (trivial), the Queensland Local Government Comparative Information report on the state open data portal for size and remoteness, and model API access. No council cooperation needed, no legal analysis, no federation explaining.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • The expected result is a clean gradient: accuracy falls with remoteness and council size, and collapses for Indigenous councils — because the training data holds a great deal about Brisbane and almost nothing about Kowanyama or Pormpuraaw. Confidently wrong is worse than refusing, and that distinction is measurable.
  • Nothing here depends on what councils are empowered to do, on the Robodebt ledger, or on any contested legal claim. It cannot be knocked over in Q&A by a correction about Australian federalism.
  • Julie's expertise is load-bearing on which questions matter and how a wrong answer actually plays out, not decorative.
  • Be honest about severity: a wrong bin day is not catastrophic. The argument is that AI is quietly becoming the default interface to government for people who can least easily reach it another way, and it degrades exactly where the alternatives are thinnest. Make that argument plainly; do not inflate it.
  • Handle carefully: if you add Aboriginal English phrasings as a second axis, present them as a method and seed set needing community validation, not a finished benchmark.
04

Ghost Warning

Prepare Difficulty: Low

“How long would it take an Australian council to detect and correct a synthetic evacuation message during a fire — and who has authority to do it at 2am?”

What you build

  • A runnable 60-minute tabletop exercise kit with timed injects: a cloned mayoral voice note in a community WhatsApp group, a fabricated emergency-services social post, an SMS with the wrong evacuation route.
  • A time-to-correct model built from real council and state emergency comms protocols — mapping who must approve a public correction, through how many steps, and what the delay is out of hours.
  • A one-page pre-authorisation template councils can adopt: the standing delegation that lets a duty officer contradict a fake without waiting for a manager.

Data required

Published state emergency management arrangements, council emergency plans (usually public), and the Bushfire Royal Commission findings on warning-message failure.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Genuinely catastrophic and genuinely local — this is a channel where AI misinformation kills people, and councils are the trusted local voice.
  • Australia has unmatched credibility on disaster comms. Excellent video demo.
  • Risk: thin as software; could land as “a Word document with a nice cover”.
  • Ethics: never generate a synthetic clip of a real, named official. Use a fictional shire throughout.
05

The Shire Floor

Prepare Difficulty: Low–Med

“What is the minimum security posture a 25-staff remote shire can actually reach — and what share of Australian councils sit below it?”

What you build

  • A capacity-calibrated baseline: not “implement the Essential Eight”, but the achievable floor for a council with one IT person, plus the AI-era additions — model access logging, vendor data flows, staff use of consumer chatbots on resident data.
  • A sector gap estimate drawn from state audit office reports on local government cyber security, which contain real published compliance numbers.
  • A self-assessment a council can complete in 30 minutes that outputs a ranked remediation list priced against a small-council budget.

Data required

NSW Audit Office and Victorian Auditor-General local government cyber audits, ACSC annual threat reports, ALGA material — all public with real numbers in them.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Grounded in published audit data, which scores well on rigor. Regional councils run water and wastewater, so the tail risk is real.
  • Weakness: crowded field, and the AI angle is speculative unless you tie it specifically to AI-uplifted attackers. Weakest inclusion story of the ten.
06

The Register

Measure Difficulty: High

“What AI is actually deployed in Australian local government right now?”

What you build

  • Start from the NSW Ombudsman's map, don't start from zero. Its March 2024 special report already catalogued 275 ADM systems across NSW agencies and councils. Nobody has updated it in over two years or replicated the method in another state — so the genuinely novel move is either a 2026 refresh of the NSW picture or the first equivalent map for Victoria or Queensland.
  • Do not attempt all 537 councils. Take a stratified random sample of 100 across metro, regional and remote. That is both achievable in a day and more defensible statistically — you can state a sector-wide estimate with an error range instead of an incomplete list.
  • Classify by risk tier and, crucially, by whether the deciding council sits under any privacy statute at all.

Data required

The NSW Ombudsman / ADM+S dataset as a baseline, then council websites, meeting minutes (PDFs of highly variable quality) and state tender portals. Free, but slow and messy.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Pure Measure track, and the “nobody has counted this since 2024” framing is strong.
  • Serious delivery risk. This is the one project on the list that can consume your entire day and produce nothing presentable. Only attempt it with a hard timebox and the sampling design above.
07

Right of Reply

Govern Difficulty: Low–Med

“If a council system makes an automated decision about you, can you actually contest it?”

What you build

  • Trace the real appeal pathway for five automated or semi-automated council decisions — infringement notices, rates arrears escalation, development applications, unapproved-structure enforcement, animal impoundment — and measure each: number of steps, reading level of the notice, channels available, time limits, and crucially whether any human in the chain has power to reverse it.
  • Build a generator that turns any such decision notice into plain language, Easy English, and spoken audio, with the appeal pathway stated as concrete next actions.

Data required

Real published council notices and appeal policies. Easy English guidelines. No acquisition problem.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • This is the central Robodebt lesson — the harm was not the algorithm, it was the absence of a working reversal path — and it is exactly Julie's professional terrain.
  • Weakness: substantially overlaps project 01. Strongest as a component of it, not a separate entry.
08

Voiceprint

Prepare Difficulty: Low

“Which communities are most exposed to AI-enabled impersonation scams routed through council-adjacent services?”

What you build

  • An exposure model combining national scam-report data by age and channel with ABS population structure by LGA, identifying where voice-cloning and impersonation scams will land hardest — rates payment redirection, aged care, home care packages.
  • A council comms kit: verification protocol, what councils should promise never to do by phone, and a warning template tested for plain language.

Data required

Scamwatch / National Anti-Scam Centre quarterly data, ABS age structure by LGA. Clean and free.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Real data, real harm, easy build, clear audience.
  • Weakness: an AI safety panel may read this as ordinary cybercrime rather than AI-driven catastrophic risk. Hardest of the ten to justify against the theme.
09

Clause Nine

Govern Difficulty: Low

“Do the contracts councils are actually signing give them the rights they would need when an AI system fails?”

What you build

  • A short library of drafted procurement clauses covering the rights councils lack: audit access, incident notification thresholds, model change notification, exit and data portability, liability for automated decisions, and a right to human review that the vendor must technically support.
  • Then the interesting half — take the publicly available terms of service for widely used government AI products and assess which clauses they would currently fail.

Data required

Public vendor terms of service, existing government procurement frameworks, state ICT panel arrangements.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Immediately usable by a decisionmaker, and the vendor gap analysis is a genuine finding rather than a template.
  • Weakness: dry to demo, and you must frame vendor assessment as analysis of published terms, carefully worded.
10

Blind Spots

Measure Difficulty: Medium

“Of the AI harms a council could plausibly cause or suffer, how many could it even detect?”

What you build

  • Enumerate roughly fifteen harm channels. For each, specify the minimum instrumentation required to notice it happening: which log, which complaint category, which staff role, which retention period, which threshold triggers escalation.
  • Assess what councils actually have, from public documents, and produce a detectability score. The answer will be low, and that inversion is the finding: the Measure track asks what councils can measure reliably, and the honest reply is “almost nothing, and here is precisely which instrument is missing in each case”.

Data required

Council complaint handling policies, records management schedules, annual reports, ombudsman reports.

Strengths & weaknesses

  • Intellectually the sharpest framing here — it is monitoring-and-evals thinking applied to an institution rather than a model, which will land well with an AI safety panel.
  • Weakness: abstract, and it needs one very concrete worked example to avoid feeling like a taxonomy exercise. The dark horse of the list.

Recommendation

The top three

Ranked by win probability for your team specifically — one working day, two people, and a panel that rewards a good question over a good build.

1

The Robodebt Test — with a small Last Mile experiment inside it

Build 01 as the spine and fold in about forty items from 03 as the evidence layer. The submission then has all four things the criteria ask for: a question (would today's frameworks have caught it?), a method (failure modes extracted from a Royal Commission and turned into criteria), an experiment (running the resulting test against a live-representative council assistant and measuring who it fails), and a tool a council can use unaided on Monday.

It is nearly data-risk-free, it is impossible for a purely technical team to produce, and the video writes itself: Australia already ran this experiment on 400,000 people. Here is the test we built from the wreckage, and here is what it catches.

Split: Julie owns the failure-mode extraction, the criteria, and the eval items. Paul owns the RAG pipeline over the report, the scoring harness, and the published site. Cut the eval to twenty items before you cut the audit.

2

Last Mile, standalone

If you want the submission to be an experiment rather than an argument, run 03 on its own and go deeper — more registers, more service domains, a proper error taxonomy. Highest novelty of anything on the list, and the resulting benchmark is the kind of artifact that outlives the weekend.

Choose this over the top pick only if you are confident you can handle the community-validation caveat gracefully on camera. Stated well it is a strength; glossed over it is the one thing that could sink an otherwise winning entry.

3

Shockmap AU

The safe, visual, quantitative option. Take it if you would rather compete on a clean data artifact than on argument, or if the map matters more to you than the finding. Be aware this is also the project most likely to be attempted by a technical team, so your edge shifts from novelty to execution — and specifically to the sensitivity analysis, which most teams will skip and which is worth more points than the map.

Dark horse: if none of these grab you, 10 (Blind Spots) is the most intellectually distinctive framing in the set and the one an AI safety panel is most likely to find genuinely interesting.

Correction · the thesis

What it actually proves

Scoring a 25-staff shire against a national welfare scheme's failure is a category error if you treat it as a compliance bar. Here is the version that survives contact with a judge.

The question that kills the weak version. “You have shown that a small rural council does not have the governance apparatus of a national agency. So what?” If the answer is a compliance score, there isn't one — small organisations lack big-organisation apparatus, and that is neither surprising nor necessarily wrong. The rubric has to stop being a bar and start being a ratio.

Separate the taxonomy from the threshold

The Royal Commission is an excellent source of what goes wrong and a bad source of how much apparatus is required. Keep the first, throw away the second. Of the failure modes, roughly four transfer cleanly to council scale — no lawful basis established before deployment, the onus of disproof shifted onto the resident, complaints not treated as a signal, and no route that can actually reverse the decision. Two transfer weakly, because they are artefacts of national scale: statistical averaging in place of individual fact, and scaling before piloting.

So score proportionality, not compliance: is the safeguard matched to the consequence of the decision this council actually automates? A shire that automates pet registration should pass easily and be told so. A shire that automates hardship assessment with no reversal pathway should fail. This is also the only version councils will adopt — a tool that tells every small shire it is failing gets deleted.

First, what an Australian council actually does

Health, education, police, hospitals, public housing, welfare payments and public transport are state functions, not council ones. Australian local government is narrow: rates, waste, local roads, development applications and building compliance, parking and local-law enforcement, animal management, environmental health, libraries, parks, cemeteries, and local emergency coordination. Any scenario that has a council assessing someone's benefit entitlement is wrong, and a judge who knows Australia will catch it.

The exception matters: Aboriginal shire councils in Queensland and regional councils in the Northern Territory deliver in remote communities what metropolitan Australians receive from the state — municipal services, sometimes essential services and housing maintenance. That is a genuine structural difference, not a rhetorical one, and it is where lived experience beats desk research.

So the risk surface is enforcement and revenue

Within those narrow powers, councils are the highest-volume issuers of small coercive automated decisions against individuals in the country — and the automation being sold to them is overwhelmingly surveillance-based:

Parking & local law
Plate recognition and automated infringement issuing. Very high volume, low individual value, rarely contested.
Aerial change detection
Commercial AI over aerial imagery flagging unapproved structures and land-use change, then driving compliance action — already in use at Australian councils across whole local government areas. Error rates will not be uniform across informal, remote or non-suburban housing.
Waste & dumping
In-truck bin contamination detection and camera-based illegal dumping identification, both producing penalties.
Rates arrears
The one genuinely severe council power. Under s 713 of the NSW Local Government Act a council may sell land where rates have been unpaid for five years; Queensland, Victoria and South Australia set the threshold at three. Councils can sell your house. That is the catastrophic tail, and it sits at the end of an escalation pipeline that is increasingly automated.

The individual harms are small; the aggregate is not. And the person who will not contest a $120 fine is precisely the person for whom $120 matters most. That is the honest severity argument, and it does not require pretending councils run a welfare system.

The catastrophic mechanism is the vendor, not the council

This is the part worth building the project around. One council failing harms hundreds of people — bad, not catastrophic. But hundreds of councils buying the same handful of vendor products means one biased model failing identically across two hundred jurisdictions at once, where no council can detect it because each sees only its own caseload, and no entity anywhere aggregates across them. Correlated failure with no observer is the shape of catastrophic risk, and unlike everything borrowed from Robodebt it is specific to local government. It is also measurable: the statutory contracts registers name the vendors.

The claim, in one sentence

The protection you get depends on which tier of government happens to decide about you — and the tier that decides most intimately is the least governed. A debt raised against you by the national agency now carries tribunal review, published decisions, an ombudsman with referral powers, and automated-decision disclosure from December 2026. The same debt raised by your council carries none of them. Same person, same harm, different desk. That is a rights claim, it is novel as a measured quantity, and it does not require anyone to know what a shire is.

Reading it from outside a federation

Assume the panel is not Australian. Shire, LGA, GIPA, PPIP and Robodebt all mean nothing to them, and every one you use costs you a sentence of explanation you cannot afford in five minutes. The underlying structure, though, is universal: a scandal at the top tier produces reform at the top tier, and the bottom tier — governed by a different level entirely — inherits none of it. That is equally true of US counties, German Kommunen, Dutch municipalities, Indian panchayats and Brazilian municípios.

Name it once, move on
“Australia's toeslagenaffaire” lands instantly with anyone in AI governance — the Dutch childcare benefits scandal wrongly accused some 26,000 families, disproportionately parents of colour and single mothers, and brought down a government. The US analogue, MiDAS, was a state system. Use one comparator, then stop explaining.
The contrast that carries it
Under Article 27 of the EU AI Act, a fundamental rights impact assessment is a deployer obligation binding bodies governed by public law — municipalities included — with notification to the market surveillance authority. A German town of three thousand deploying a high-risk system owes that assessment. An Australian council deploying the identical system owes nothing to anyone. Check the Annex III commencement date before you cite it — the high-risk timetable has been amended and is currently slated for late 2027.
Measuring stick, not model
Do not propose that Australian councils adopt the EU regime — a full fundamental rights impact assessment is absurd apparatus for a 25-staff shire, and recommending it would be bad advice a judge will spot. Borrow the classification questions, which are portable, and leave the compliance machinery, which is not. The deliverable is “here is what would be classified high-risk in Europe, is unregulated here, and here is a shire-sized version of the assessment” — not “adopt the AI Act”.
Open with this
“In most countries the government closest to you — the one that fines you, houses you, and decides whether you get help — is the least regulated user of AI in the state. We measured how much.” No jurisdiction-specific vocabulary, and every judge already knows whether it is true where they live.

The actual thing to build

Council Scan

Not a report about councils — an instrument that assesses any council automatically, from documents that council is legally required to publish.

Why NSW, and why there is enough data

NSW is the only jurisdiction where the corpus is mandated, uniform and per-council. 128 councils, one legal regime, and three statutory publication duties that hand you a comparable document set for every single one:

PPIP Act s 33
Every council must have a Privacy Management Plan covering its information practices and its internal review procedures. Published. This is your primary document.
GIPA Act ss 6, 18, 27
Every council must publish a contracts register for contracts of $150,000 or more, within 45 days. This is where the AI vendors are.
OLG “Your Council”
Downloadable comparative dataset for all 128 councils — staffing, revenue, expenditure, demographics. Your structured baseline and your capacity variable.
NSW Ombudsman map
275 ADM systems catalogued across NSW agencies and councils in March 2024. Your ground truth for known deployments, and your 2024 comparison point.

The rubric — eight checks, each traceable

Every criterion has to satisfy two tests: it traces to a numbered Royal Commission finding, and it is checkable from a public document. That intersection is what keeps this from being opinion. Score each one against the consequence of the decision, not against a fixed bar — the same missing reversal pathway is a footnote on pet registration and a serious finding on hardship assessment.

Refuse to publish a league table. A composite score invites the only real attack available — “your weighting is arbitrary” — and it is a fair attack. Publish per-criterion findings with a quoted passage and a source URL for each, and make your one headline figure a count: how many of 128 councils publish any disclosure of automated decision-making at all. Counts are defensible. Indices are not. Saying this out loud in the video is worth more than the index would have been.

What makes it a build, not a slide deck

A

Finding, not scraping

There is no standard URL for a privacy management plan. Across 128 sites it lives under a different name, menu and file type each time. An agent that searches, reads and decides “this is the document” is doing work a scraper cannot, and work that was infeasible before tool-using models.

B

Judgement at scale

Deciding whether a complaints policy offers genuine reversal or merely a response is a reading task with a rubric, applied 128 times across eight criteria. That is a classification pipeline with a defined ground truth — an eval, in the ordinary AI-safety sense.

C

Measure your own instrument

Hand-label 15 councils between you, then report your pipeline's agreement with human coding, per criterion. You stop asserting scores and start reporting a measurement instrument's accuracy — including where it is unreliable. Almost no hackathon team will do this.

The limitation, and the reframe that survives it. Not published does not mean does not exist — a council may do the right thing and never write it down, and you must say so plainly. But recommendation 17.1 asked specifically that websites carry the disclosure, because a review right nobody can find is not a review right. So publication is not a proxy for the thing you care about; for transparency it is the thing. State the limit, then state why the measure still holds.

Shape of it

Pipeline
Async fetch → document discovery → PDF and HTML extraction → per-criterion classification → evidence store. Cache every fetch to disk so reruns cost nothing; you will rerun many times.
Models
A cheap fast model for the bulk classification pass across 128 × 8 judgements; a stronger one to adjudicate only where confidence is low or where your hand labels disagree. Ask for structured output and store the quoted evidence span with every verdict.
Store
SQLite is plenty for a day. Reach for Supabase only if the front end needs to read live.
Front end
One input: pick a council. One output: a scorecard, eight rows, each with a verdict, a quoted passage and a link to the source document. Then a sector view behind it.
The demo
Type a council name, get a cited scorecard in twenty seconds. Then the sector count. Then your agreement-with-human-coding figure. That sequence — instrument, finding, accuracy — is the whole submission.
If time runs short
Cut councils before you cut criteria, and cut criteria before you cut the hand-labelled validation. Thirty councils scored honestly beats 128 scored unverifiably.

Execution

One day, honestly budgeted

Times are AEST, assuming you work Saturday and reserve Sunday morning for the video. Verify the actual window when you register.

Thu–Fri, before
Register. Read the Royal Commission executive summary and recommendations. Pick your project and write the one-sentence question down. Do not write code yet.
Sat 08:00–09:00
Lock scope. Write the question, the claim you expect to make, and the limitations section — before you have results. If the claim turns out wrong, that is a finding, and having written it down first is what makes it rigorous.
Sat 09:00–12:30
Core artifact. Extraction and criteria, or pipeline and data join. Parallel tracks: one person on content, one on code, syncing on the half hour.
Sat 12:30–15:00
Run it against something real. Whatever your project, this is the step that separates you from the field. Score real frameworks, real councils, a real assistant. Record the raw results.
Sat 15:00–17:00
The site. Static page, no auth, no database. Findings first, method second, tool third, limitations visible not buried. Publish it and get the URL working early.
Sat 17:00–18:30
Write the limitations page properly. This is a scored criterion and almost every team treats it as an afterthought. Name what you could not check, what your sample cannot support, and what would change your conclusion.
Sun 08:00–11:00
Video. Script it — five minutes is short. Thirty seconds on the question, ninety on the method, two minutes on the finding with the artifact on screen, thirty seconds on limitations, thirty on what a council does with it. Record it twice.
Sun 11:00–13:00
Buffer. Something will break. If nothing breaks, tighten the writing — communication is a quarter of the score.
The one habit worth more than any feature. Every claim on your site should be either linked to a source or labelled as an estimate with its method shown. Judges scoring “rigor and honesty” are looking for exactly this, and it costs you almost nothing to do while you build. A page where the reader can check you is worth more than a page with twice the content.
